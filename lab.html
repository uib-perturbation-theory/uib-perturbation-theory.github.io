Interactive gravity

Black Hole Lab

Six small animations explain black-hole physics visually: ringdown, accretion, lensing, spacetime distortion, binary coalescence and interactive perturbations. Four simulators go further: one solves the Regge–Wheeler equation live in your browser, another follows a body falling into a rotating black hole, a third sends waves through a wormhole between two universes, and the fourth sends messages, sounds and pictures through it.

01 · Ringdown

Quasinormal-mode ringing

A perturbed black hole relaxes like a struck bell. The shadow is deformed in a quadrupolar pattern and the deformation decays with the Schwarzschild fundamental-mode ratio ωR/ωI ≈ 4.2.

02 · Accretion

Relativistic beaming

The inclined disk is brighter on the approaching side and dimmer on the receding side. It is not a full ray-traced simulation, but it captures the basic visual idea behind Doppler boosting.

03 · Lensing

Starfield lensing

Background stars are displaced and stretched around the shadow, hinting at primary and secondary images near an Einstein ring.

04 · Shader

The page bends around the hole

A WebGL fragment shader bends a texture as if the black hole were distorting the light from the page itself. Move the cursor over the panel to drag the lens.

05 · Binary coalescence

Inspiral, merger and ringdown

The last six orbits of SXS:BBH:0305, a numerical-relativity simulation of a binary like that of GW150914: two black holes inspiral, merge and leave behind a ringing remnant. The holes follow the apparent horizons of the simulation, and the strip below shows its gravitational wave, seen face-on, changing from a chirp to a damped sinusoid.

Data: SXS Collaboration, SXS:BBH:0305 (CC BY 4.0).

06 · Interactive perturbation

Poke the black hole

Use the cursor as a passing star. Moving close to the hole or clicking on the panel excites a damped quadrupolar oscillation and launches gravitational-wave ripples.

07 · Simulator

Regge–Wheeler simulator

Send a Gaussian pulse onto a Schwarzschild black hole and watch it scatter in 3D. The Regge–Wheeler equation is solved in your browser with the numerical set-up of our Shaping black hole resonances papers. Choose the pulse and the observer, and compare the ringing with the quasinormal-mode prediction.

Open the simulator →
08 · Simulator

Sasaki–Nakamura simulator

Watch a body fall into a Kerr black hole along its real trajectory, down the rotation axis or spiralling in from the innermost stable orbit, in the equatorial plane or on a tilted orbit, and see the gravitational waves and the ringdown it leaves behind. The waves come from the generalized Sasaki–Nakamura equation. Change the spin and compare the ringing with the Kerr quasinormal modes.

Open the simulator →
09 · Simulator

Wormhole simulator

Two black-hole exteriors glued along a thin shell form a wormhole between two universes. Send a wave packet through it and watch it split: part is reflected, part is trapped between the two potential barriers and leaks out as echoes, and part comes out on the other side. Change how close the throat is to the horizon and see the cavity, the echoes and the transmission comb change.

Open the simulator →
10 · Simulator

Talking through a wormhole

Send a digital message, an SOS in Morse, a sound or a picture through a thin-shell wormhole and see what a receiver in the other universe gets. Above the potential barrier the message arrives intact; below it, only a comb of narrow lines gets through, and their ringing smears the bits. Change the wave, the throat and the mass of the wormhole and listen to the result.
