Talking through a wormhole

Someone far away in one universe sends a message towards the throat of a wormhole. A receiver far away in the other universe records what comes out. Send a digital message, an SOS in Morse, a sound or a picture, and see what arrives. The throat filters every signal the same way: it is transparent above the top of the potential barrier and, below it, lets signals through only on a comb of narrow lines. Everything is computed in your browser from the exact transmission of the throat.

The physics behind this

The wormhole

Take the outside of a Schwarzschild black hole of mass $M$ down to a radius $a$ just outside where the horizon would be, make a second copy and glue the two along that sphere. This is the thin-shell wormhole of Visser, held open by a shell of exotic matter at the throat. We write

$$ a = 2M(1+\delta). $$

Each universe has the potential barrier of a black hole, peaked near $r = 3M$. When the throat lies inside the peak, the two barriers face each other and form a cavity, the cavity that produces the echoes of the wormhole simulator. The three throats offered here are an ultracompact one ($\delta = 10^{-6}$), a short one ($\delta = 10^{-2}$) and one outside the peak ($\delta = 0.85$, $a = 3.7M$), with a single barrier and no cavity.

The throat is a filter

Since the wormhole does not change with time, a signal sent from one universe arrives in the other filtered by a fixed transmission amplitude $T(\omega)$: if the sender emits the waveform $x(t)$, the receiver records

$$ y = \mathcal F^{-1}\!\left[\,T(\omega)\,\mathcal F x\,\right], \qquad T(\omega) = \frac{i\omega}{q\,h}, $$

where $q$ and $h$ are the value and slope (corrected by the jump at the shell) of the purely outgoing solution at the throat. The transmission depends only on $M\omega$, so the mass just slides the whole picture in frequency. For the electromagnetic dipole the barrier top is at $M\omega = 0.272$: about $8.8$ kHz for a wormhole of one solar mass, in the audible band, and $2.9$ GHz for one of the mass of the Earth.

Above the barrier top the throat is almost transparent. Below it the cavity acts as a Fabry–Pérot interferometer whose mirrors are the two black-hole barriers: waves get through only on a comb of narrow lines, the quasinormal modes of the throat, where $|T|^2$ reaches 1. The widths of the lines are not free. They are fixed by the absorption $T_{\rm BH}$ of the black hole of the same mass,

$$ \frac{\pi\Gamma_n}{\Delta\omega_n} \simeq \frac{T_{\rm BH}}{2 - T_{\rm BH}}, $$

with $\Gamma_n$ the width of line $n$ and $\Delta\omega_n$ the spacing of the comb, so that, averaged over the comb and where $T_{\rm BH}$ is small, the wormhole lets through half of what the black hole would absorb. In the paper this law is checked on 835 lines of scalar, electromagnetic and gravitational waves.

What happens to a message

  • Above the barrier a message arrives intact, through any throat. With smooth bits, every bit of “SOS” is decoded for $M\nu \ge 0.28$, and in noise the wormhole costs at most $0.1$ dB for $M\nu \ge 0.42$.
  • Below the barrier an ultracompact throat smears the bits. At $M\nu = 0.2$ and $\delta = 10^{-6}$ the received wave carries 20% of the energy and keeps ringing after the message has ended, and even without noise a receiver that decides bit by bit gets only 22 of the 24 bits. In noise this leaves an error floor that no transmitted power removes. A receiver that knows $T$ and decodes a whole frame at once removes it, at the cost of 10–30 dB more energy per bit.
  • An SOS in Morse gets through only on a comb line that rings down in about a dot or less. On a 700 Hz tone a solar-mass ultracompact throat passes $|T|^2 \sim 10^{-12}$ and only noise is heard. On the line at 5.86 kHz, which rings for 8 ms, less than a dot, the call is read. On the line at 4.02 kHz, which rings for 79 ms, the dots blur but the call is still read. On the lowest line, at 2.05 kHz, which rings for 2.4 s, the energy crosses but the call merges into one slowly rising tone.
  • Pictures arrive with ghosts. Below the barrier each echo of the cavity leaves a shifted copy of the picture, one round trip of the cavity later (slightly more than the light round trip $4d$ between the two peaks).

How long the channel lasts

No thin-shell wormhole of this kind that is symmetric under the exchange of the two universes is stable: polar gravitational perturbations that are odd under that exchange grow, for every throat radius. Near the black-hole limit, however, they grow slowly, by a factor $e$ in about $7M/\delta$: 35 s for a solar mass at $\delta = 10^{-6}$, the time to send about $7\times10^4$ of the smooth bits used here. Electromagnetic waves do not excite this mode at linear order. Throats outside the photon sphere, like the $\delta = 0.85$ one, grow in about $60M$, less than one bit, so they would need something outside the model to hold them.

How this page computes

  • $T(M\omega)$ was computed with the solver of the paper on a grid that is dense on every comb line, for $10^{-4} \le M\omega \le 2$. Outside that range the page uses $T \propto \omega^{\ell+1}$ below and $|T| = 1$ above, checked against the solver.
  • The received wave is $\mathcal F^{-1}[T\,\mathcal F x]$ with a fast Fourier transform. For the digital message it reproduces the waves of the paper to better than 1% of the peak, the energy to 2% or better, and the fidelity and delay exactly. The decoded bits agree, except in borderline cases where a single bit can differ.
  • Digital message: binary phase-shift keying (each bit flips the sign of the carrier), with 100M bits and raised-cosine edges half a bit long, or 40M bits with 10M edges. The receiver finds the delay from the cross-correlation with the sent wave, correlates each bit window with the carrier and takes the preamble as the phase reference. $E_b$ is the sent energy per bit, so the loss in the throat appears as a lower signal-to-noise ratio. With noise the page shows one random draw. The bit-error rates of the paper are computed exactly instead.
  • Morse: “SOS SOS” at 20 words per minute (60 ms dots) with 5 ms edges. The receiver adds fixed white noise of 2% of the tone amplitude, keeps a 120 Hz band around the tone and thresholds its envelope at half height.
  • Pictures travel as in analogue television: line by line, one sample per $M$, each pixel's brightness modulating the amplitude of the carrier. The receiver detects the envelope and aligns it with the sent one.

Things to try

  • Digital message: move the carrier from $0.5$ down to $0.1$ with the ultracompact throat, then with $\delta = 0.85$. The throat without a cavity loses energy but keeps the bits; the ultracompact one rings.
  • Switch to 40M sharp bits at $M\nu = 0.2$: the sharp edges spread the spectrum above the barrier, and the message gets through again.
  • SOS: step through the comb lines and compare how long each one rings with the 60 ms of a dot.
  • Sound: play the sweep through the ultracompact throat. Below the barrier top you hear only the comb lines, and the lowest one keeps ringing for seconds after the sweep has passed it.
