Black Hole Lab · Simulator

Wormhole simulator

Two copies of the outside of a black hole, glued along a thin shell just outside where the horizon would be, form a wormhole between two universes. Send a wave packet at it from one side: part of it bounces back, part of it gets trapped between the two potential barriers and leaks out as echoes, and part of it comes out in the other universe. Everything is solved live in your browser.

The surface is a slice through the wormhole that contains the symmetry axis of the multipole. Universe 1 is the upper sheet and universe 2 the lower one; they meet at the throat. Distance along the surface away from the throat is the tortoise coordinate |x|, so waves travel at constant speed, and the slope follows the Flamm paraboloid of each side. It is a picture, not an exact embedding. The wave pushes the surface along its normal by ψ(t, x) Pℓ(cos θ). Both observers sit on the axis. Units: G = c = M = 1.

The theory behind this

A wormhole made of two black-hole exteriors

Take the Schwarzschild geometry of a black hole of mass $M$, keep only the region outside a sphere of radius $a$ slightly larger than the horizon, make a second copy, and glue the two along that sphere. This is the thin-shell wormhole of Visser: the gluing surface is the throat, and it needs a thin shell of exotic matter (negative energy density) to hold it open. We write

$$ a = 2M(1 + \delta), $$

so that $\delta$ measures how close the throat is to the would-be horizon. Seen from far away, each side looks exactly like a black hole of mass $M$. The difference is that nothing is absorbed: what falls in on one side comes out on the other.

The wave equation

A single coordinate $x$ runs through both universes: $x > 0$ is universe 1, $x < 0$ universe 2 and $x = 0$ the throat. It is the tortoise coordinate measured from the throat,

$$ r_*(r(x)) = r_*(a) + |x|, \qquad r_* = r + 2M\ln\!\left(\frac{r}{2M} - 1\right), $$

inverted with the Lambert W function, as in the Regge–Wheeler simulator. Scalar ($s = 0$), electromagnetic ($s = 1$) and axial gravitational ($s = 2$) waves of multipole $\ell$ all obey

$$ \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} - \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} + V\psi = 0, \qquad V = \left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)\left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2} + \frac{2M(1 - s^2)}{r^3}\right]. $$

Each universe has the potential barrier of a black hole, peaked near $r = 3M$. If the throat is inside the peak, the two barriers face each other across the throat at $x = \pm d$, with $d = r_*(r_{\rm peak}) - r_*(a) \approx 2M\,|\ln\delta|$. Between them lies a cavity. It only grows logarithmically: even $\delta = 10^{-10}$ gives a cavity about $90M$ long.

The shell

At the throat the wave must be continuous, but its slope can jump, because the shell is a sheet of matter and the radius $r(x)$ has a kink there. The shell acts as a delta-function potential,

$$ \psi'(0^+) - \psi'(0^-) = C\,\psi(0), \qquad C = \frac{2f(a)}{a}\times\{+1,\ 0,\ -1\}\ \ \text{for}\ \ s = \{0,\ 1,\ 2\}, $$

with $f = 1 - 2M/r$. For a scalar field it follows from the kink of $r(x)$, for electromagnetic waves it vanishes, and for axial gravitational waves it follows from the Israel junction conditions, as for thin-shell gravastars. Since $|C| = \delta/(1+\delta)^2$, it is small for throats close to the horizon. The arrow at the throat in the potential panel shows its sign.

Echoes and transmission

A pulse sent from universe 1 first meets the outer barrier. What is reflected there reaches observer A exactly as if the wormhole were a black hole: the two cannot be told apart until the first echo. What tunnels through enters the cavity and bounces between the two barriers. Each time it hits one of them, a little leaks out: back to universe 1 as an echo, or forward to universe 2. The echoes arrive roughly once per round trip of the cavity.

In frequency, the cavity acts like a Fabry–Pérot interferometer whose mirrors are the two barriers. Above the barrier top the wormhole is almost transparent. Below it, waves only get through at the resonances of the cavity, a comb of narrow lines spaced by about $\pi/2d$, where the transmission reaches 1. The lines are the quasinormal modes of the wormhole, and they get narrower, and decay more slowly, the lower their frequency.

The numerical method

Both solvers run in background threads of your browser (Web Workers).

  • Time domain: grid $x \in [-600,\,600]\,M$ with spacing $\Delta x = 0.2\,M$ (6,001 points), fourth-order finite differences and the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method with $\Delta t = 0.25\,\Delta x$, up to $t = 800\,M$. The shell enters as a discrete delta, $C/\Delta x$ added to the potential at the node $x = 0$. The potential is switched off smoothly for $|x| > 450\,M$, and the grid is large enough that nothing reflected at its ends comes back to the region shown.
  • The initial pulse is an oscillating Gaussian moving towards the throat, $\psi = e^{-(x - x_0)^2/2\sigma^2}\cos\nu(x - x_0)$ and $\partial_t\psi = \partial_x\psi$.
  • Energy. The energy density $\tfrac12(\dot\psi^2 + \psi'^2 + V\psi^2)$ is conserved, with flux $-\dot\psi\,\psi'$. The numbers under the traces are the energy beyond each observer: what was there at $t = 0$ plus what has crossed it since, as a fraction of the total.
  • Transmission: we write the solution that is purely outgoing in universe 1 as $j = e^{i\omega x}A(x)$, start $A$ from its asymptotic series in $1/r$ at $r \ge \max(250M, 25/\omega)$ and integrate it in to the throat with fourth-order Runge–Kutta, with fine steps around the cavity. With $q = j(0)$ and $h = j'(0) - Cq/2$, continuity and the jump at the shell give $|T|^2 = \omega^2/|qh|^2$. Lines narrower than the frequency grid are located from jumps in the phase of $q$ or $h$ and refined to their peak.
  • The percentage quoted under the transmission is $\int |T|^2 E(\omega)\,d\omega / \int E(\omega)\,d\omega$, with $E(\omega) \propto \omega^2|\tilde\psi_0(\omega)|^2$ the energy spectrum of the pulse. It should agree with the energy beyond observer B at the end of the run to about a per cent: some energy may still be trapped in the cavity, and the pulse starts inside the tail of the potential rather than infinitely far away.

Things to try

  • Raise the carrier frequency above the barrier top: the pulse crosses almost untouched, and there are no echoes.
  • Lower it below the top and make the pulse wide (large σ), so that its spectrum is narrow. Most of it is reflected, and what crosses comes out as long ringing at the frequencies of the comb lines.
  • Move δ towards $10^{-10}$: the cavity, the tube in the 3D view and the time between echoes all grow, and the comb lines move closer together.
  • Set δ to 1: the throat is now outside the peak, there is a single barrier and no cavity, and nothing comes back after the first reflection.
