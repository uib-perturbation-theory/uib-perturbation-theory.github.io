Sasaki–Nakamura simulator

A small body falls from rest along the rotation axis of a Kerr black hole. On its way in it sends out a burst of gravitational waves, and the black hole then rings at its quasinormal frequencies. The waves come from the generalized Sasaki–Nakamura equation; the trajectory is computed in your browser.

The theory behind this

The set-up

A body of mass $\mu \ll M$ starts at rest very far away, so its energy per unit mass is $E = 1$, and falls along the rotation axis of a Kerr black hole of mass $M$ and spin $a$. It moves on a geodesic. With $\Delta = r^2 - 2Mr + a^2$, in Boyer–Lindquist coordinates,

$$ \frac{dr}{d\tau} = -\frac{\sqrt{2Mr\,(r^2 + a^2)}}{r^2 + a^2}, \qquad \frac{dt}{d\tau} = \frac{r^2 + a^2}{\Delta}. $$

Because the motion is along the axis, the problem is axisymmetric and only modes with $m = 0$ are excited. The simulator shows the strongest one, $\ell = 2$. Since $\mu \ll M$, the body is a small perturbation and its waves can be computed on the fixed Kerr background.

The Sasaki–Nakamura equation

Gravitational waves on Kerr are described by the Teukolsky equation for the Weyl scalar $\psi_4$. After separating variables, its radial part has a long-range potential, and for a body that comes in from infinity the source grows without bound at large $r$, which makes it hard to integrate. Sasaki and Nakamura found a change of variables to a function $X$ that obeys an equation with short-range potentials,

$$ \frac{d^2 X}{dr_*^2} - \mathcal F(r)\,\frac{dX}{dr_*} - \mathcal U(r)\,X = \mathcal S, \qquad \frac{dr_*}{dr} = \frac{r^2 + a^2}{\Delta}. $$

The functions $\mathcal F$ and $\mathcal U$ depend on the frequency $\omega$, on $a$ and on the eigenvalue of the spheroidal harmonics. Far from the hole the solutions behave as $e^{\pm i\omega r_*}$, like free waves. For $a = 0$ the equation becomes the Regge–Wheeler equation of our other simulator. We use its generalized form (GSN) due to Lo, which works for any spin weight of the field.

For each frequency, the outgoing amplitude $X(\omega)$ at infinity is an integral of the solution that is purely ingoing at the horizon against the source of the falling body, divided by a Wronskian. The source carries a factor $e^{i\omega v(r)}$ that follows the body, with $v = t + r_*$ its advanced time.

From frequencies to the waveform

The signal at the observer is

$$ \Psi(u) = \frac{1}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Re}\int_0^\infty X(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega u}\,d\omega, \qquad u = t - r_*. $$

Far from the hole, $\Psi$ differs from the strain of the gravitational wave only by a known factor at each frequency, so it carries the same information.

The waveforms were computed by the group for $a = 0, 0.1, \dots, 0.9$, following the set-up of the GSN plunge paper and the GeneralizedSasakiNakamura.jl code. As a check, the frequency of the ringing read off from them agrees with that of Kerr's fundamental mode to better than one per cent.

The trajectory in the animation

The time origin is the one of the waveforms: the body crosses the horizon at advanced time $v = 0$. Along its path,

$$ \frac{dv}{dr} = -\frac{r^2 + a^2}{2r\,(1 + s)}, \qquad s = \sqrt{\frac{r^2 + a^2}{2r}}, \qquad t_{\rm p}(r) = v(r) - r_*(r), $$

which your browser integrates from the horizon outwards. The animation starts when the body is at $r = 40\,M$. The sheet shows $\Psi(t - r_*)$, the wave that will reach a distant observer, only where it already exists: outside the body, since the waves move outwards faster than it falls, and outside the light ring, the unstable photon orbit near $r = 3M$ from which the ringing leaks out. In the tortoise coordinate the body reaches the edge of the drawn hole in a finite time. In the coordinate time $t$ it never quite crosses the horizon, but its light becomes exponentially redshifted.

The ringdown and the tail

After the peak, the wave contains damped oscillations at the quasinormal frequencies $\omega_n$ of the Kerr black hole. The frequencies are those tabulated by Berti, Cardoso and Starinets. For $m = 0$ they depend only mildly on the spin, as the panel of quasinormal frequencies above shows.

On top of the ringing there is a tail that does not oscillate. It comes from the branch cut of $X(\omega)$ along the negative imaginary frequency axis, $\Psi_{\rm tail}(u) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \mathrm{Im}\,X(-i\gamma)\,e^{-\gamma u}\,d\gamma$. For a body that falls from infinity it is large, about 15% of the peak $10\,M$ after it, and it decays only as a power of $u$. It is the source of the uneven zero crossings of the wave, and at late times it is all that is left.

The dashed blue curve on the observer panel is a least-squares fit, done in your browser, of

$$ \Psi(u) \simeq \sum_n 2\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left[C_n\, e^{-i\omega_n \tau}\right] + \sum_{k=1}^{5} b_k\, e^{-\gamma_k \tau}, \qquad \tau = u - u_{\rm peak}, $$

with $n = 0, \dots, 3$, the frequencies $\omega_n$ fixed to the Kerr values and only the amplitudes $C_n$ and $b_k$ free. The fit starts $5\,M$ after the peak and runs up to $110\,M$ after it. The exponentials, with fixed rates $\gamma_k$ between $0.005$ and $0.25$, stand in for the tail integral, which is itself a sum of decaying exponentials; their sum is the dotted grey curve. With all four modes the fit matches the wave to better than $10^{-3}$ of the peak. The $n = 0$ button keeps only the fundamental mode and the tail: close to the peak this misses the overtones, which decay faster, so it is only drawn from $25\,M$ after the peak, once they have died out.

The spectrum

At low frequencies $|X(\omega)|$ grows roughly as $\omega^{-1/3}$, the imprint of the slow, Newtonian part of the fall. Above the quasinormal frequencies it drops exponentially, as Davis, Ruffini, Press and Price first found in 1971 for a body falling into a Schwarzschild black hole. A faster spin raises the quasinormal frequencies and makes the hole ring for longer.
