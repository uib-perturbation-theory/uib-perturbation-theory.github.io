Regge–Wheeler simulator

A Gaussian pulse of gravitational waves falls onto a Schwarzschild black hole. Part of it is reflected by the potential barrier and rings at the quasinormal frequencies; the rest falls through the horizon. The equation is solved live in your browser.

The sheet is a slice through the black hole that contains the symmetry axis. Its height is the Regge–Wheeler function Ψ(t, r*) times the angular pattern Pℓ(cos θ), drawn against the tortoise coordinate r*, so the waves travel at constant speed. The observer sits on the axis. Units: G = c = M = 1.

The theory behind this

The equation

Small odd-parity perturbations of a Schwarzschild black hole of mass M are described, for each multipole ℓ, by a single function Ψ(t, r*) that obeys the Regge–Wheeler equation

$$ \left(-\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2} + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial r_*^2} - V_\ell(r)\right)\Psi = 0, \qquad V_\ell(r) = \left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)\left(\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2} - \frac{6M}{r^3}\right). $$

The tortoise coordinate $r_* = r + 2M \ln(r/2M - 1)$ sends the horizon to $r_* \to -\infty$. In this coordinate the waves move at the speed of light everywhere, and the black hole is just a potential barrier with its peak near $r = 3M$, that is, $r_* \approx 1.6M$. To go back from $r_*$ to $r$ the code uses the Lambert W function, $r = 2M\,[1 + W(e^{r_*/2M - 1})]$.

The initial pulse

At $t = 0$ the field is an oscillating Gaussian centred at $r_{*0}$ that moves towards the black hole:

$$ \Psi(0, r_*) = e^{-(r_* - r_{*0})^2/2\sigma^2}\cos\!\big(\nu\,(r_* - r_{*0})\big), \qquad \partial_t \Psi(0, r_*) = \partial_{r_*}\Psi(0, r_*). $$

The width σ and the carrier frequency ν set which frequencies the pulse carries. Its spectrum, the orange curve in the right-hand panel, is $|\tilde\Psi_0(\omega)| = \tfrac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2}\,\sigma\,\big[e^{-\sigma^2(\omega-\nu)^2/2} + e^{-\sigma^2(\omega+\nu)^2/2}\big]$.

The numerical method

The solver is a JavaScript translation of the code used in the Shaping black hole resonances papers and runs in a background thread of your browser (a Web Worker). It agrees with the original Python code to single precision.

  • Grid: $r_* \in [-400.2,\,1500]\,M$ with 8,942 points and spacing $\Delta r_* = 1700/7999\,M \approx 0.21\,M$. This is the papers' grid on $[-200, 1500]\,M$, extended to the left with the same spacing.
  • Fourth-order finite differences in $r_*$, with one-sided stencils at the two ends of the grid.
  • Time integration with the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method, $\Delta t = 0.1\,\Delta r_*$: 32,937 steps up to $t = 700\,M$.
  • The potential is switched off smoothly near the horizon, between $r = 2.01M$ and $2.2M$, and far away, between $r = 500M$ and $1000M$.
  • No boundary conditions are imposed. The grid is large enough that nothing reflected at its ends comes back to the observer before $t = 700\,M$.
  • The observer's signal is read with fourth-order Lagrange interpolation between the four nearest grid points.

The quasinormal-mode prediction

At late times the signal is a sum of damped oscillations at the quasinormal frequencies $\omega_n$ of the black hole. The papers give the amplitude of each one in closed form, from the excitation factors $B_n$ of the black hole and the overlap $T_n$ of the pulse with the mode:

$$ \Psi_{\rm QNM}(t) = \sum_n 2\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left[C_n\, e^{-i\omega_n (t - r_*^{\rm obs} - r_{*0})}\right], \qquad C_n = B_n T_n, $$ $$ T_n = -i\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma\,\omega_n\left[e^{-\sigma^2(\omega_n - \nu)^2/2} + e^{-\sigma^2(\omega_n + \nu)^2/2}\right]. $$

The factor 2 accounts for the mirror modes $-\omega_n^*$. The frequencies and the excitation factors $B_n$ are those tabulated by Berti and Cardoso (2006). The map shows $|C_n|$. A narrow pulse carries a broad range of frequencies and excites the mode moderately for any ν. A wide pulse excites it strongly when its carrier frequency is close to $\mathrm{Re}\,\omega_n$ (the dashed line on the map) and hardly at all otherwise.

The dashed blue curve on the observer panel is $\Psi_{\rm QNM}$, drawn from the peak of the scattered signal onwards. The formula only describes the late part of the signal. For the fundamental mode $n = 0$ of $\ell = 2$ it matches the simulation to a few per cent once the ringing dominates. It is less accurate where $|C_n|$ is strongly suppressed. For overtones of wide pulses the factor $e^{\sigma^2(\mathrm{Im}\,\omega_n)^2/2}$ makes the predicted amplitudes very large, and they only describe the signal much later. For this reason the $n \le 3$ curve starts once the overtones have decayed below the peak of the signal.

The spectrum

The dark curve is the Fourier transform of the observer's signal from the moment the incident pulse has passed until the end of the run. The ratio of the two curves is close to the reflectivity of the barrier, $|R(\omega)|$. Low frequencies are reflected and high frequencies cross into the black hole, and the change happens close to the quasinormal frequencies. Where the scattered signal is more than about a hundred times weaker than the pulse, the ratio also picks up details of the numerical set-up, such as the smooth cut-off of the potential near the horizon.
