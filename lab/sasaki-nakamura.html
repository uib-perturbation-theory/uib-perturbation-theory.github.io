Sasaki–Nakamura simulator

A small body falls into a Kerr black hole: from rest along the rotation axis, or spiralling in from the innermost stable orbit, in the equatorial plane or tilted from it. On its way in it sends out gravitational waves, and the black hole then rings at its quasinormal frequencies. The waves come from the generalized Sasaki–Nakamura equation; the trajectory is computed in your browser.

The theory behind this

The simulator follows a body of mass $\mu \ll M$ on one of two geodesics of a Kerr black hole of mass $M$ and spin $a$: a fall from rest along the rotation axis, and a plunge from the innermost stable circular orbit in the equatorial plane. Since $\mu \ll M$, the body is a small perturbation and its waves can be computed on the fixed Kerr background.

The fall along the axis

The body starts at rest very far away, so its energy per unit mass is $E = 1$. With $\Delta = r^2 - 2Mr + a^2$, in Boyer–Lindquist coordinates,

$$ \frac{dr}{d\tau} = -\frac{\sqrt{2Mr\,(r^2 + a^2)}}{r^2 + a^2}, \qquad \frac{dt}{d\tau} = \frac{r^2 + a^2}{\Delta}. $$

Because the motion is along the axis, the problem is axisymmetric and only modes with $m = 0$ are excited. The simulator shows the strongest one, $\ell = 2$.

The Sasaki–Nakamura equation

Gravitational waves on Kerr are described by the Teukolsky equation for the Weyl scalar $\psi_4$. After separating variables, its radial part has a long-range potential, and for a body that comes in from infinity the source grows without bound at large $r$, which makes it hard to integrate. Sasaki and Nakamura found a change of variables to a function $X$ that obeys an equation with short-range potentials,

$$ \frac{d^2 X}{dr_*^2} - \mathcal F(r)\,\frac{dX}{dr_*} - \mathcal U(r)\,X = \mathcal S, \qquad \frac{dr_*}{dr} = \frac{r^2 + a^2}{\Delta}. $$

The functions $\mathcal F$ and $\mathcal U$ depend on the frequency $\omega$, on $a$ and on the eigenvalue of the spheroidal harmonics. Far from the hole the solutions behave as $e^{\pm i\omega r_*}$, like free waves. For $a = 0$ the equation becomes the Regge–Wheeler equation of our other simulator. We use its generalized form (GSN) due to Lo, which works for any spin weight of the field.

For each frequency, the outgoing amplitude $X(\omega)$ at infinity is an integral of the solution that is purely ingoing at the horizon against the source of the falling body, divided by a Wronskian. The source carries a factor $e^{i\omega v(r)}$ that follows the body, with $v = t + r_*$ its advanced time.

From frequencies to the waveform

The signal at the observer is

$$ \Psi(u) = \frac{1}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Re}\int_0^\infty X(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega u}\,d\omega, \qquad u = t - r_*. $$

Far from the hole, $\Psi$ differs from the strain of the gravitational wave only by a known factor at each frequency, so it carries the same information.

The waveforms of the fall along the axis were computed by the group for $a = 0, 0.1, \dots, 0.9$, following the set-up of the GSN plunge paper and the GeneralizedSasakiNakamura.jl code. As a check, the frequency of the ringing read off from them agrees with that of Kerr's fundamental mode to better than one per cent.

The fall in the animation

The time origin is the one of the waveforms: the body crosses the horizon at advanced time $v = 0$. Along its path,

$$ \frac{dv}{dr} = -\frac{r^2 + a^2}{2r\,(1 + s)}, \qquad s = \sqrt{\frac{r^2 + a^2}{2r}}, \qquad t_{\rm p}(r) = v(r) - r_*(r), $$

which your browser integrates from the horizon outwards. The animation starts when the body is at $r = 40\,M$. The sheet shows $\Psi(t - r_*)$, the wave that will reach a distant observer, only where it already exists: outside the body, since the waves move outwards faster than it falls, and outside the light ring, the unstable photon orbit near $r = 3M$ from which the ringing leaks out. In the tortoise coordinate the body reaches the edge of the drawn hole in a finite time. In the coordinate time $t$ it never quite crosses the horizon, but its light becomes exponentially redshifted.

The ringdown and the tail

After the peak, the wave contains damped oscillations at the quasinormal frequencies $\omega_n$ of the Kerr black hole. The frequencies are those tabulated by Berti, Cardoso and Starinets. For $m = 0$ they depend only mildly on the spin, as the panel of quasinormal frequencies above shows.

On top of the ringing there is a tail that does not oscillate. It comes from the branch cut of $X(\omega)$ along the negative imaginary frequency axis, $\Psi_{\rm tail}(u) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \mathrm{Im}\,X(-i\gamma)\,e^{-\gamma u}\,d\gamma$. For a body that falls from infinity it is large, about 15% of the peak $10\,M$ after it, and it decays only as a power of $u$. It is the source of the uneven zero crossings of the wave, and at late times it is all that is left.

The dashed blue curve on the observer panel is a least-squares fit, done in your browser, of

$$ \Psi(u) \simeq \sum_n 2\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left[C_n\, e^{-i\omega_n \tau}\right] + \sum_{k=1}^{5} b_k\, e^{-\gamma_k \tau}, \qquad \tau = u - u_{\rm peak}, $$

with $n = 0, \dots, 3$, the frequencies $\omega_n$ fixed to the Kerr values and only the amplitudes $C_n$ and $b_k$ free. The fit starts $5\,M$ after the peak and runs up to $110\,M$ after it. The exponentials, with fixed rates $\gamma_k$ between $0.005$ and $0.25$, stand in for the tail integral, which is itself a sum of decaying exponentials; their sum is the dotted grey curve. With all four modes the fit matches the wave to better than $10^{-3}$ of the peak. The $n = 0$ button keeps only the fundamental mode and the tail: close to the peak this misses the overtones, which decay faster, so it is only drawn from $25\,M$ after the peak, once they have died out.

The spectrum

At low frequencies $|X(\omega)|$ grows roughly as $\omega^{-1/3}$, the imprint of the slow, Newtonian part of the fall. Above the quasinormal frequencies it drops exponentially, as Davis, Ruffini, Press and Price first found in 1971 for a body falling into a Schwarzschild black hole. A faster spin raises the quasinormal frequencies and makes the hole ring for longer.

The plunge from the innermost stable orbit

In the second trajectory the body moves in the equatorial plane, in the same sense as the hole rotates. Circular orbits there are stable down to the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO), at $r = 6M$ for $a = 0$ and closer to the horizon for faster spins. The body starts on it and keeps the energy $E$ and angular momentum $L$ per unit mass of that orbit. With $P = E(r^2 + a^2) - aL$,

$$ \frac{dr}{dt} = -\frac{\sqrt{R}}{T}, \qquad \frac{d\varphi}{dt} = \frac{aP/\Delta - (aE - L)}{T}, $$ $$ R = P^2 - \Delta\left[r^2 + (L - aE)^2\right], \qquad T = \frac{(r^2 + a^2)\,P}{\Delta} - a\,(aE - L). $$

$R$ has a triple root at the ISCO, so the body takes an infinite time to leave it: it circles slowly inwards for ever before it plunges. Your browser integrates these equations, with $t = 0$ and $\varphi = 0$ when the body crosses the light ring of the plunge, the prograde circular photon orbit at $r = 2M\left[1 + \cos\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\arccos(-a/M)\right)\right]$. The animation starts at $t = -200\,M$. Close to the horizon the hole drags the body around with it: $d\varphi/dt$ tends to the angular velocity of the horizon, $\Omega_H = a/(2Mr_+)$, while in the tortoise coordinate the body keeps falling at the speed of light.

Waves from the plunge

The plunge is not axisymmetric, and the strongest waves have $\ell = 2$ and $m = \pm 2$. For each frequency the amplitude of the outgoing wave is an integral along the orbit,

$$ Z_\omega = \frac{1}{2i\omega\,B^{\rm inc}_\omega}\int dt\; e^{i(\omega t - 2\varphi(t))}\, I_\omega\big(r(t)\big), $$

where $I_\omega$ combines the solution of the radial Teukolsky equation that is purely ingoing at the horizon, and its first two derivatives, with the projections of the body's stress–energy tensor on the Kinnersley tetrad (Sasaki and Tagoshi), and $B^{\rm inc}$ is the incidence amplitude of that solution. The radial solutions were computed with GeneralizedSasakiNakamura.jl, which solves the GSN equation and transforms its solution into the Teukolsky function. The $\ell = m = 2$ mode of the strain, and the strain in the equatorial plane, are

$$ r\,h_{22}(u) = -2\int d\omega\,\frac{Z_\omega}{\omega^2}\,e^{-i\omega u}, \qquad h_+ = 2\sqrt{\frac{5}{64\pi}}\;\mathrm{Re}\!\left[h_{22}\,e^{2i\varphi}\right], \qquad h_\times = 0, $$

since for an orbit in the equatorial plane $h_{2,-2} = h_{22}^*$. The observer panel shows $r h_+/\mu$ in that plane. As checks, for circular orbits the amplitudes agree with the point-particle routine of the same code to one part in $10^7$, and early in the animation the wave of the plunge has the amplitude of the circular orbit the body is on to $0.2\%$. Because the body has been circling for ever, its source is switched on smoothly between $t = -480\,M$ and $-360\,M$, early enough that the transient has died away before the part of the wave shown.

The ringdown of the plunge

The ringing is at the $m = 2$ quasinormal frequencies, which grow strongly with the spin: the light ring moves closer to the horizon and turns faster. Its frequency and decay rate agree with Kerr's fundamental mode to better than one per cent for all the spins. Just after the peak, though, the wave is not yet a sum of modes, since the body still radiates as it is dragged around near the horizon. The fit on the observer panel therefore starts $15\,M$ after the peak of $|dh_{22}/du|$, which marks the plunge better than the peak of $|h_{22}|$, and uses the modes $n = 0, \dots, 3$ with no tail, which is small here. It matches the wave to better than $10^{-3}$ of the peak; the fundamental mode alone is drawn from $30\,M$ after the peak.

The spectrum of the plunge has a spike at twice the orbital frequency of the ISCO, where the body spends most of its time; its height grows with the length of that phase, which here is set by the switch-on. The plunge fills the band from there up to the quasinormal frequency, above which the spectrum falls off exponentially, as for the fall along the axis.

Tilted orbits

Off the equatorial plane a geodesic of Kerr has a third constant of motion besides $E$ and $L$, the Carter constant $Q$, and there are spherical orbits, which stay at a fixed $r$ while the body swings up and down in latitude and the orbital plane precesses around the spin axis. In the third trajectory the body starts on the innermost stable one, the ISSO. Its tilt is the inclination $\iota$, with $x = \cos\iota$ positive for orbits that go round with the hole: the body reaches latitudes $\pm\iota$, so $|\cos\theta| \le z_- = \sin\iota$. For $a = 0$ the inclination is only a rotation and the ISSO is the ISCO at $6M$; for $a = 0.9\,M$ its radius goes from $2.32\,M$ at $\iota = 0^\circ$ to $8.72\,M$ at $\iota = 180^\circ$.

With the Mino time $\lambda$, defined by $dt/d\lambda = \Sigma\,dt/d\tau$ with $\Sigma = r^2 + a^2\cos^2\theta$, the radial and polar motions separate. Writing $z = \cos\theta = z_-\cos\chi$,

$$ \left(\frac{dr}{d\lambda}\right)^2 = R(r) = P^2 - \Delta\left[r^2 + (L - aE)^2 + Q\right], \qquad \frac{d\chi}{d\lambda} = \sqrt{\frac{Q}{z_-^2} - a^2(1 - E^2)\,z_-^2\cos^2\chi}, $$ $$ \frac{dt}{d\lambda} = \frac{(r^2 + a^2)\,P}{\Delta} + aL - a^2E\,(1 - z^2), \qquad \frac{d\varphi}{d\lambda} = \frac{aP}{\Delta} - aE + \frac{L}{1 - z^2}. $$

At the ISSO $R$ again has a triple root. Your browser finds $E$, $L$ and $Q$ by solving $R = R' = R'' = 0$ together with the condition that fixes the inclination, and then integrates the plunge in $t$, with $t = 0$ and $\varphi = 0$ when the body crosses the prograde photon orbit, as before. Where the body is in its up-and-down motion at that moment is the phase $\chi_0$: at the top of its swing, on the way down, at the bottom or on the way up. The last two are the mirror images of the first two in the equatorial plane, which turns $h_{\ell m}$ into $(-1)^\ell\,h_{\ell,-m}^*$, so only two phases were computed. At $\iota = 90^\circ$ the body has $L = 0$ and passes over the poles.

Waves from a tilted plunge

Now every $m$ is excited, and the body moves through the angular pattern of each mode. The amplitudes become

$$ Z_{\ell m\omega} = \frac{1}{2i\omega\,B^{\rm inc}_{\ell m\omega}}\int dt\; e^{i(\omega t - m\varphi(t))}\, I_{\ell m\omega}\big(r(t), \theta(t)\big), $$

where the source $I_{\ell m\omega}$ now carries the spin-weighted spheroidal harmonic ${}_{-2}S_{\ell m}(\theta; a\omega)$ and its first two derivatives along $\theta(t)$, and terms with the polar velocity that vanish in the equatorial plane. We use the source for generic orbits of Sasaki and Tagoshi, in the form given by Drasco and Hughes with the corrections of their 2014 erratum. A radial solution at $(\ell, m, \omega)$ also gives the one at $(\ell, -m, -\omega)$ by complex conjugation, so each solve serves two modes. To let the observer move, the spheroidal harmonics are expanded in spherical ones, ${}_{-2}S_{\ell m} = \sum_{\ell'} b^{\ell m}_{\ell'}(a\omega)\,{}_{-2}Y_{\ell' m}$, and

$$ r\,h_{\ell' m}(u) = -2\sum_{\ell}\int d\omega\,\frac{Z_{\ell m\omega}}{\omega^2}\, b^{\ell m}_{\ell'}(a\omega)\,e^{-i\omega u}, \qquad h_+ - i h_\times = \sum_{\ell' m} h_{\ell' m}(u)\;{}_{-2}Y_{\ell' m}(\theta, \varphi), $$

with $\ell, \ell' = 2, 3, 4$ and every $m$. Your browser does the last sum for the direction of the observer, and the sheet shows the same sum in the equatorial plane. On the spin axis only $m = 2$ (above) or $m = -2$ (below) reaches the observer, since ${}_{-2}Y_{\ell m}$ vanishes there for every other $m$. The frequency integrals run up to $|\omega| = 2.5/M$ in steps of $0.003/M$ and are rolled off smoothly above $1.6$ times the fundamental quasinormal frequency of each mode. The source is switched on as the body starts to leave the ISSO, and never later than $t = -480\,M$, over at least $120\,M$ and almost three orbits, so that the transient has gone before the part of the wave shown.

As checks, for $\iota = 0^\circ$ the amplitudes agree with those of the equatorial plunge to $10^{-9}$; the source reproduces the amplitudes of the spherical-orbit routine of GeneralizedSasakiNakamura.jl, including $m \le 0$ and the harmonics of the polar motion; at $a = 0$ the power in each $\ell$ does not depend on the inclination, to $10^{-8}$, as it must for a rotated orbit; and the mirror symmetry holds to $10^{-13}$. The ISSO and the orbital frequencies agree with KerrGeodesics.jl to twelve digits or better.

The ringing now mixes many modes. The counter-rotating ones, with $m < 0$ in the plane at the bottom of the page, are excited strongly when the orbit goes against the rotation, and the observer sees each $m$ with a different weight. The fit on the observer panel uses the fundamental modes of $\ell = 2$ and $3$ with every $m$ (and adds the first overtones of $\ell = 2$ for "n ≤ 1"), starting $15\,M$ after the peak of the total flux $\sum_{\ell m} |dh_{\ell m}/du|^2$, which does not depend on the observer. It matches the wave to about $10^{-3}$ of its peak.

The volume view

The sheet shows the wave in one plane. The volume view shows it in every direction at once: at each point around the hole, at distance $r_*$ and angles $(\theta, \varphi)$, it evaluates

$$ h_+ = \mathrm{Re}\sum_{\ell m} h_{\ell m}(t - r_*)\;{}_{-2}Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \varphi), $$

with the same modes as the observer, and draws it as a glowing cloud, orange where $h_+ > 0$ and blue where $h_+ < 0$, more opaque where $|h_+|$ is large. As on the sheet the $1/r$ fall-off is left out, so a wave keeps its brightness as it travels out. Your browser tabulates the angular sums on a grid in $r_*$ and $\theta$ every frame and marches along each line of sight through a ball around the hole, adding up colour and opacity. The scale is linear in $h_+$: the compressed heights of the sheet would make the long, steady parts of the signal fill the ball. In the fall along the axis the pattern is $\Psi\,\sin^2\theta$ around the line of fall; in the equatorial plunge it is the $\ell = 2$, $m = \pm 2$ pair, a double spiral that is strongest along the spin axis; in the tilted plunge every $m$ adds its own twist.
